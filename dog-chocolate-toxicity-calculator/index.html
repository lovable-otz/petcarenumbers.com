Dog Chocolate Toxicity Calculator

If your dog ate chocolate, call your veterinarian, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 or the Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661 now. Call before using this tool. This chocolate dog toxicity calculator estimates the theobromine and caffeine dose in mg/kg and compares it with Merck Veterinary Manual thresholds. It cannot tell you any amount is safe.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Pet Care Numbers

My dog ate chocolate: what should I do right now?

Call now, whatever the calculator says: your veterinarian, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661. Both poison lines are open 24 hours a day. The ASPCA line is open 365 days a year, and Pet Poison Helpline 24/7.

Both services charge for a consultation. The ASPCA says a consultation fee may apply. Pet Poison Helpline says an $89 incident fee applies and all follow-up consultations are included. Both pages were checked on September 16, 2026.

While you call:

  • Don’t wait for signs. Merck says signs usually appear 6–12 hours after a dog eats chocolate, so a dog that seems fine now may not stay that way.
  • Don’t treat your dog at home. Pet Poison Helpline says not to give home antidotes, and not to induce vomiting without first consulting a vet or the helpline.
  • Keep the wrapper. The type of chocolate, the cocoa percentage, the amount and the time your dog ate it all help the experts. Other ingredients, such as raisins, nuts or xylitol, can add their own dangers.

Pet Poison Helpline notes that the sooner a poisoning is addressed, the easier, cheaper and safer it is to treat.

Why is chocolate toxic to dogs?

Chocolate contains two stimulants, theobromine and caffeine, both methylxanthines. According to the Merck Veterinary Manual (full review September 2024, updated February 2026), they block the body’s adenosine receptors. This overstimulates the nervous system, increases urination and speeds up the heart.

Merck adds that they may raise levels of adrenaline (epinephrine) and norepinephrine.

Dogs clear these compounds slowly. Merck gives a half-life in dogs of 17.5 hours for theobromine and 4.5 hours for caffeine. Merck also says dogs are the animals most often affected, because they eat almost anything and chocolate is easy for them to reach. Chocolate’s high fat content is a separate risk: Merck notes it may trigger pancreatitis in animals prone to it.

The calculator adds theobromine and caffeine together, because Merck’s figures for chocolate products combine them.

Which chocolate is most dangerous for dogs?

The darker and less sweetened the chocolate, the more theobromine and caffeine it contains. Cocoa powder and baking chocolate are the most concentrated, and white chocolate has very little. The figures below are from the Merck Veterinary Manual. Real products vary by brand and cocoa bean.

For a bar labeled with a cocoa percentage, Merck multiplies 15.5 mg/g by that percentage. A 65% bar comes to about 10.1 mg/g. The calculator uses the same method.

Here is how much the product matters. One ounce of a 70% bar eaten by a 15 lb dog comes to about 308 mg, or 45 mg/kg, which is in Merck’s heart-effects range. Two ounces of milk chocolate eaten by a 20 lb dog is about 130 mg, or 14 mg/kg. That is below the level where Merck says mild signs may begin, but products and dogs vary, so the advice is still to call.

What are the signs of chocolate poisoning in dogs?

Merck says signs usually appear within 6–12 hours. The first signs are increased thirst, vomiting, diarrhea, a swollen belly and restlessness. They can progress to hyperactivity, more frequent urination, unsteady walking (ataxia), stiffness, tremors and seizures. If you notice any of these, call your vet now.

The Merck Veterinary Manual also lists these signs:

  • a fast heart rate or extra heartbeats (premature ventricular contractions)
  • rapid breathing and cyanosis (a bluish tint from low oxygen)
  • high blood pressure and high body temperature
  • in some dogs, a slow heart rate, low blood pressure or coma
  • low potassium late in the illness, which adds to heart problems

Merck says signs can last up to 72 hours in severe cases, and deaths are generally caused by abnormal heart rhythms, overheating or breathing failure.

Merck links the dose to the kind of signs:

Theobromine + caffeineMerck description
20 mg/kgMild signs (vomiting, diarrhea, increased thirst)
40–50 mg/kgHeart effects
60 mg/kg and aboveSeizures

Merck adds that severe signs and death may occur at doses much lower than the reported lethal dose. That is one reason the calculator never labels any result as safe.

How do vets treat chocolate poisoning?

Treatment depends on how much your dog ate, how long ago, and what signs it shows. Your veterinarian or the poison-control team decides. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes these parts of veterinary care:

  • Decontamination: if the dog ate the chocolate recently, the vet may consider making the dog vomit, using medicines given in the clinic. Merck says activated charcoal should only be considered for exposures that could be lethal, and only when the benefits outweigh the risks.
  • Controlling signs: medicines to stop vomiting, to control tremors or seizures, and to correct a heart rate that is too fast or too slow.
  • Fluids: Merck says fluid therapy increases urine output and may help stabilize the heart and circulation. For dogs that can’t walk, vets may place a urinary catheter so less of the compounds is reabsorbed.
  • Monitoring: heart tracing (ECG), blood chemistry and body temperature.

None of this is something to try at home. Your vet or poison-control expert will tell you what your dog needs. If you have a cat that ate chocolate, call the same numbers: Merck says many species can be affected, but this calculator’s thresholds are for dogs.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate chocolate toxicity in a dog?

First, call your vet or a poison line. The calculation is grams eaten × mg of theobromine and caffeine per gram (from Merck), divided by the dog’s weight in kg. Compare the result with Merck’s 20, 40–50 and 60 mg/kg levels. The calculator does this for you, but it can’t rule out harm.

How do I flush chocolate out of my dog’s system?

Don’t try to do this at home. Pet Poison Helpline says not to give home antidotes and not to induce vomiting without consulting a vet or the helpline. Vets may use clinic-given medicines to make the dog vomit, fluids and other care, as Merck describes. Call (888) 426-4435 or (855) 764-7661.

How can I tell if my dog has chocolate poisoning?

Merck lists increased thirst, vomiting, diarrhea, a swollen belly and restlessness first. More serious signs include hyperactivity, tremors, seizures and a racing or irregular heartbeat. Signs usually start 6–12 hours after eating, so call your vet as soon as you know your dog ate chocolate, before signs appear.

How many grams of chocolate can make a dog sick?

Merck says mild signs can occur at 20 mg/kg. That level is reached with about 8.7 g of milk chocolate, 1.3 g of baking chocolate or 0.7 g of cocoa powder per kg of body weight. Products and dogs vary, so a smaller amount is not proof of safety. Call anyway.
