Dog Food Calculator

A 30 lb spayed or neutered adult dog needs about 794 calories a day by the veterinary formula in the Merck Veterinary Manual (updated September 2024). That is 1.6 × its resting energy requirement. This dog food calculator uses the calories printed on your food’s label to turn that figure into cups, cans or grams.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Pet Care Numbers

How many calories does my dog need a day?

Start with the resting energy requirement (RER): 70 × body weight in kg raised to the power 0.75. Then multiply by a life-stage factor. Merck’s table uses 1.6 for a neutered adult, 1.8 for an intact adult and 1.4 for a dog that gains weight easily.

A 30 lb dog weighs 13.61 kg. Its RER is 70 × 13.61^0.75 = 496 kcal. Multiplied by 1.6, that gives 794 kcal a day. The Ohio State University Veterinary Medical Center uses the same method. Its example is a 10 kg neutered dog with an RER of about 400 kcal. The calculator gets 394, which works out to 630 kcal a day.

These numbers are only a starting point. Merck says a dog may need up to 30% more or fewer calories than another dog of the same weight. OSU says individual needs can differ from the calculation by as much as 50%. For an active or working dog, you choose a factor from 2 to 5. Start low and adjust with your veterinarian.

How do I convert calories into cups of food?

Divide your dog’s daily calories by the kcal per cup printed on the bag. Most labels give a calorie statement per kilogram and per cup or can. For the 30 lb dog above, leave 10% of the calories for treats and feed the rest from a food with 380 kcal per cup: 714 ÷ 380 = 1.88 cups a day, or 0.94 cups per meal over two meals.

For raw or fresh food labeled in kcal per kilogram, the tool gives grams instead. For example, a 20 kg working dog on a factor of 5 needs about 3,310 kcal. At 1,500 kcal/kg, that is about 2,207 g a day.

Why the feeding chart on the bag may give a different number

A bag’s chart lists amounts by weight alone. It can’t know whether your dog is neutered, how active it is or whether it gains weight easily, and those are the things the Merck factors account for. Both are estimates. The 2021 AAHA Nutrition and Weight Management Guidelines say what decides the right amount: whether your dog keeps a healthy body condition score. Weigh your dog every few weeks, and ask your vet to check its body condition at each visit.

How much should I feed a puppy?

Puppies need more calories per pound than adults. Merck uses 3 × RER for puppies under 4 months and 2 × RER from 4 months until adulthood. A 5 kg puppy under 4 months needs about 702 kcal a day, which the calculator can split across three meals.

What a puppy eats matters as much as how much. The AAHA guidelines (J Am Anim Hosp Assoc 2021;57:153–174) advise feeding a diet made for growth until the skeleton is mature. For small and medium dogs, that is usually around 1 year. Some large and giant breeds are not mature until closer to 15–16 months. For those breeds, AAHA advises keeping the puppy lean and avoiding too much calcium.

A puppy’s weight changes every week, so recalculate often. Your veterinarian should confirm the amounts, especially for large and giant breeds. The puppy weight calculator shows the average young-adult weight for 72 breeds.

How many treats can my dog have?

Treats and other extras should make up no more than 10% of daily calories, and the complete diet at least 90%. That is the guidance in the 2021 AAHA guidelines. For the 30 lb dog in the example, 10% is about 79 kcal a day.

The calculator sets that 10% aside by default and works out the food portion from the other 90%. Untick the box if your dog gets no treats. AAHA notes that feeding more than 10% of calories from foods that are not complete and balanced dilutes the nutrients your dog needs and adds extra calories.

Count everything. AAHA points out that pet food labels must state calories, and so must treat labels, but chews, bones and toys are not covered. Food used to hide pills and supplements adds calories too.

How do I feed a dog that needs to lose weight?

Weight loss should be planned with your veterinarian, starting from the ideal weight your vet sets. OSU’s table uses 1.0 × the RER of that ideal weight. For a dog whose ideal weight is 25 kg, that is about 783 kcal a day.

Choose “Weight loss” in the calculator and enter the ideal weight, not your dog’s current weight. Cutting back a regular food too far can leave a dog short of nutrients. Merck explains that weight-management diets are made less energy dense, with nutrients adjusted to match. A therapeutic weight-loss diet may be needed to prevent deficiencies when calories are cut hard.

AAHA recommends reassessing and adjusting the plan at each visit. Don’t start a weight-loss plan on your own for a dog that is pregnant, nursing, still growing or ill. Those dogs need their portions set by a veterinarian. For a cat, use the cat food calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how much food to give my dog?

Work out the RER (70 × weight in kg^0.75), multiply by the life-stage factor, then divide by the calories per cup on the label. A 30 lb neutered dog eating 380 kcal/cup food, with 10% saved for treats, gets about 1.88 cups a day. Adjust as your vet advises.

How much should a dog eat per day by weight?

For spayed or neutered adults on the Merck factor of 1.6, the formula gives about 348 kcal a day at 10 lb, 794 at 30 lb, 1,164 at 50 lb and 1,498 at 70 lb. Divide by your food’s kcal per cup to get cups. Individual needs can vary by up to 50%.

What is the 90/10 rule for dog treats?

The 2021 AAHA guidelines say a complete and balanced diet should make up at least 90% of calories, with treats and other extras at 10% or less. The calculator uses this split. It is not a rule for splitting two main foods.

Can I use a calculator to balance a homemade dog food recipe?

A calorie calculator can’t tell you whether a recipe is balanced. AAHA’s 2021 guidelines note that studies found most home-prepared recipes from books and websites were not complete and balanced, even some written by veterinarians. AAHA recommends asking a board-certified veterinary nutritionist to formulate one.
